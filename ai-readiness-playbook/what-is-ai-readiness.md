# What Is AI Readiness?

## Introduction

Artificial Intelligence has become a priority for many organizations. New copilots, automation tools, and AI-powered solutions are being introduced across almost every industry.

However, moving from experimentation to measurable business value is often more difficult than expected.

In many organizations, the challenge is not the AI technology itself. The real challenge is that data, governance, ownership, and business processes are not sufficiently mature to support AI at scale.

From my experience working on enterprise data and analytics initiatives, successful AI adoption depends far more on strong foundations than on selecting a particular AI platform or model.

AI readiness is the ability of an organization to use its data, knowledge, processes, and people effectively to support AI-driven decision making and business outcomes.

---

## A Common Misconception

When organizations begin exploring AI, the conversation often starts with technology.

Questions typically include:

- Which AI model should we use?
- Which platform should we invest in?
- Should we buy a solution or build our own?
- How quickly can we deploy AI capabilities?

While these are important considerations, they are rarely the best starting point.

A more useful question is:

**Are we ready to trust the outputs generated by AI?**

Answering this requires a deeper understanding of data quality, governance, ownership, and business context.

---

## AI Readiness Starts with Trust

AI systems learn from and depend on enterprise data.

If that data is incomplete, inconsistent, or poorly understood, the results will reflect those same issues.

Before introducing AI at scale, organizations should be able to answer questions such as:

- Do we trust the data used in our reports and dashboards?
- Are important business metrics calculated consistently?
- Do we understand where data originates?
- Is ownership clearly defined?
- Can employees easily find and understand available information?
- Are governance processes established and followed?

Organizations that can answer these questions confidently are usually in a much stronger position to adopt AI successfully.

## Lessons from Enterprise Data Programs

Throughout my career, I have worked on data and analytics initiatives across retail, manufacturing, healthcare, and life sciences organizations.

Although the industries were different, similar patterns often emerged.

### IKEA

While supporting food inventory and enterprise data warehouse solutions, the focus was not simply on producing reports.

Significant effort was spent establishing trusted data, improving data quality, and creating consistency across business processes.

The same foundational capabilities become even more important when organizations begin introducing AI-driven insights and recommendations.

### Stellantis

Following the merger of FCA and PSA, analytics teams needed to work across multiple systems, processes, and reporting approaches.

Before advanced analytics could deliver value, business stakeholders needed confidence in common definitions, ownership models, and data consistency.

This is a challenge many organizations encounter when scaling AI across business units.

### Johnson & Johnson Vision Care

Regional analytics platforms supported commercial and financial reporting across multiple markets.

Success depended on clear governance, standardized business rules, and strong data controls.

These same capabilities are often required before AI solutions can be trusted by business users.

---

## AI Readiness Is Not Just a Technology Challenge

AI readiness requires collaboration between business and technology teams.

Business teams provide:

- Strategic priorities
- Business outcomes
- Operational knowledge
- Decision-making context

Technology teams provide:

- Data platforms
- Integration capabilities
- Analytics solutions
- Security controls

Governance teams provide:

- Data standards
- Compliance requirements
- Risk management
- Policies and controls

AI readiness exists when these groups are working towards a shared objective rather than operating independently.

---

## Characteristics of an AI-Ready Organization

Organizations that are ready for AI typically demonstrate several common characteristics.

### Trusted Data

- Data quality issues are identified and addressed proactively.
- Reports are viewed as reliable sources of information.
- Business users have confidence in the data they consume.

### Clear Ownership

- Critical data assets have defined owners.
- Responsibilities are understood across business and technology teams.
- Decisions can be made without lengthy debates about accountability.

### Consistent Business Definitions

- Key business metrics are calculated consistently.
- Teams use a shared business vocabulary.
- Reporting disputes are reduced.

### Strong Governance

- Policies are understood and followed.
- Security and compliance requirements are managed appropriately.
- Governance supports business objectives rather than creating unnecessary obstacles.

### Discoverable Knowledge

- Users can find relevant information quickly.
- Data assets are documented.
- Metadata and lineage provide business context.

### Alignment with Business Goals

- AI initiatives are connected to measurable business outcomes.
- Success is measured in business impact rather than technical activity.
- Investments are prioritized based on value.

---

## AI Readiness Is a Journey

Very few organizations become AI-ready overnight.

Some organizations have modern platforms but weak governance.

Others have strong governance but fragmented data.

Most organizations are strengthening different capabilities at different speeds.

The objective is not perfection.

The objective is to continuously improve the foundations that make AI adoption sustainable and scalable.

---

## Why Readiness Matters

Organizations that invest in AI readiness often see benefits long before large-scale AI adoption.

These benefits may include:

- Higher confidence in reporting
- Improved data quality
- Better collaboration between business and technology teams
- Clear ownership and accountability
- Faster access to information
- More effective decision making
- Reduced duplication of effort

In many cases, these improvements generate value regardless of whether AI initiatives are immediately implemented.

---

## My Perspective

One observation has remained consistent across many of the programs I have supported.

Organizations rarely struggle because they selected the wrong analytics platform, cloud provider, or AI tool.

More often, challenges arise because:

- Data exists across disconnected systems.
- Business definitions differ between teams.
- Ownership is unclear.
- Governance processes are inconsistent.
- Valuable organizational knowledge is difficult to discover.

AI tends to make these challenges more visible rather than eliminate them.

For this reason, I view AI readiness as a business capability supported by technology rather than a technology initiative supported by the business.

---

## Key Takeaways

- AI readiness begins with trusted data.
- Strong governance creates confidence and accountability.
- Consistent business definitions reduce confusion and improve alignment.
- Ownership is just as important as technology.
- Metadata and business context play a critical role in AI adoption.
- The same foundations that support analytics also support AI.
- Organizations that invest in readiness are better positioned to scale AI successfully.
